\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why hierarchical pruning works here}
The results of Section~\ref{sec:results} are driven by a property of the design space
rather than by a clever search order: in a heterogeneous edge--cloud CPS the vast
majority of candidate architectures is either ill-formed, or violates a hard constraint
that is already implied by a handful of early decisions, or is dominated by a design that
is cheaper in every objective. On S3 only $34\,560$ of $51\,840$ candidates are even
well-formed, of those a further $7\,000$ to $12\,700$ survive the feasibility bounds, and
of those fewer than 60 need to be evaluated. Every candidate removed at those layers would
otherwise consume a full simulation.

The ablation makes the mechanism explicit. Dominance pruning is the stronger of the two
bound-based layers, but it is only effective once an archive of good designs exists;
feasibility pruning removes precisely the sub-trees in which no such design will ever be
found, so it reduces the number of tree nodes at which the dominance test has to be
evaluated. The combination is roughly an order of magnitude stronger than either layer
alone.

\subsection{When HCA-DSE is the right tool --- and when it is not}
The method is advantageous when the design space is large, the constraints are tight, the
evaluator is expensive, and admissible bounds can be constructed. The crossover analysis
makes the third condition quantitative: with the analytical evaluator, whose cost is
comparable to that of the bounds themselves, the wall-clock gain is only
$1.3$--$3.3\times$ despite a $99.9\%$ reduction in evaluations, whereas with a
discrete-event evaluator the same reduction turns into two orders of magnitude. Since
industrial dCPS exploration reports evaluation costs of up to a
minute per design point~\cite{saadatmand2025compdse}, the practically relevant regime is
firmly on the favourable side of the crossover.

Conversely, the method offers little when the space is small, the constraints are loose,
the evaluation is nearly free, or --- most importantly --- when no admissible bound can be
constructed for the objectives of interest. If a bound cannot be proven admissible it must
not be used: the guarantee of Theorem~\ref{thm:exact} rests entirely on that property, and
an inadmissible bound silently converts an exact method into an approximate one without
any indication in the output.

\subsection{Relation to symbolic exact exploration}
Symbolic approaches~\cite{neubauer2018exact} prune partial designs with a constraint
solver and are, in principle, more general than closed-form bounds: any constraint the
solver can express can participate in the pruning. The price is that the objectives must
be assignment monotonous for dominance checks on incomplete assignments to be sound. Our
construction trades generality for applicability to contention-dependent objectives:
Lemma~\ref{lem:exact} together with the decision ordering of Remark~\ref{rem:order}
recovers exactness for a latency model with queueing, at the cost of bounds that must be
derived per objective. The two directions are complementary, and combining a solver-based
front end with contention-aware bounds is a natural next step.

\subsection{Relation to evaluator-centric methodologies}
CompDSE~\cite{saadatmand2025compdse} and HCA-DSE answer different questions. The former
asks how a design point of an industrial dCPS can be modelled and evaluated faithfully;
the latter asks which design points need not be evaluated at all. Their combination is
straightforward in principle --- the analytical model of Section~\ref{sec:model} would
serve as the bound generator and the trace-derived simulator as the evaluator --- and it
is the configuration in which our crossover analysis predicts the largest benefit.

\subsection{Accuracy versus ordering}
The empirical validation supports a narrower claim than "the model is accurate". The
model's absolute error is $16$--$50\%$ depending on the workload, which is in the same
range as the $20\%$ execution-time deviation reported for a design-time placement tool
validated on a real deployment~\cite{sedghani2024space4aid}. What the exploration actually
requires, however, is that the model orders architectures correctly, and that claim is
supported much more strongly: $98\%$ agreement on the pairs the model separates by more
than $25\%$. The complement is equally informative --- within a single workload the
rank correlation is lower, because after the M/D/1 correction many designs of one workload
differ by less than the measurement noise. A model that cannot separate near-equivalent
architectures is not a defect of the exploration method but an argument for the two-level
scheme: analytical bounds remove the bulk of the space, and the surviving handful of
candidates is where an expensive high-fidelity evaluator should be spent.
