\subsection{RQ1: most architectures are discarded before evaluation}
Table~\ref{tab:funnel} and Fig.~\ref{fig:funnel} report the exploration funnel. On S3
($51\,840$ candidates) HCA-DSE performs 41 to 54 full evaluations depending on the
workload, a reduction of $99.90\%$ with respect to the raw space; on S4
($345\,600$ candidates) it performs 52 to 60, a reduction of $99.98\%$. The composition
of the funnel differs by workload: for data-intensive sensing the feasibility bounds
remove the largest share, whereas for telemetry dominance pruning does most of the work,
which reflects the different binding constraints of the two regimes.

\subsection{RQ2: the exact front is preserved}
For every scale for which exhaustive enumeration is tractable (S1--S3) and for every
workload, the set of objective vectors returned by HCA-DSE is identical to the exhaustive
Pareto front: recall and precision are both $1.0$. The same holds for each ablated
variant, confirming that no individual pruning layer is unsafe. Bound admissibility was
verified exhaustively on S1 --- all prefixes against all completions --- without a single
violation, and for every pruned prefix on S1 and S2 all completions were enumerated and
found consistent with the claim of the corresponding proposition.
Fig.~\ref{fig:pareto} shows the resulting front for S3/telemetry in two projections.

\subsection{RQ3: when pruning pays off}
Using the measured overheads and evaluation counts in \eqref{eq:breakeven}, the
break-even evaluator cost is $1$--$3\,\mu$s on S2 and below $1\,\mu$s on S3, i.e.\ any
evaluator more expensive than a few floating-point operations already justifies the
pruning overhead. Fig.~\ref{fig:crossover} shows the speed-up over exhaustive enumeration
as a function of $C_E$. At the measured cost of the discrete-event evaluator
($19.4$\,ms) the predicted speed-up is $78\times$ on S2 and $558\times$ on S3, and it
saturates at the ratio of evaluation counts, $81\times$ and $640\times$ respectively. With
the cheap analytical evaluator the measured speed-up is only $1.3$--$3.3\times$, because
then the bound computation dominates the runtime; this is exactly the regime in which
pruning is \emph{not} the right tool, and we report it rather than omit it.

\subsection{RQ4: comparison with budget-matched exploration}
Table~\ref{tab:baselines} reports the comparison over 20 seeds. On S3 NSGA-II reaches a
median Pareto recall of $0.50$--$0.74$ and an HV ratio of $0.94$--$0.98$ only at a budget
of $1000$ full evaluations, while random search never recovers a single exact front point
at that scale. HCA-DSE attains recall, precision and HV ratio of $1.0$ with 41 to 54
evaluations, i.e.\ between $18\times$ and $24\times$ fewer evaluations than the best
budget of the stochastic baseline. At the smaller S2 scale NSGA-II is competitive in
hypervolume ($0.999$ at $B=1000$) but still misses $9$--$22\%$ of the exact front points.
Fig.~\ref{fig:hv_budget} shows the seed-level distributions.

The comparison should be read as a statement about guarantees, not about the quality of
evolutionary search: NSGA-II converges towards the front and would reach it with more
budget, but it cannot certify that anything is missing, whereas HCA-DSE returns the exact
front and does so at a small fraction of the evaluation budget --- provided admissible
bounds exist.

\subsection{Ablation: what each layer contributes}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} and Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation} isolate the layers on S3. Structural
pruning alone leaves the full $34\,560$ well-formed candidates to be evaluated.
Feasibility bounds alone reduce this to $7\,025$--$12\,700$ ($2.7\times$--$4.9\times$).
Dominance bounds alone are far more aggressive, leaving $327$--$415$ evaluations, but the
two are complementary: combined they leave 41 to 54, i.e.\ another $6\times$--$10\times$
beyond dominance alone, because feasibility pruning removes exactly the sub-trees for
which the dominance test would otherwise have to be repeated deep in the tree. All
variants preserve the exact front.

\subsection{RQ5: scalability}

\subsection{RQ6: transfer to a real deployment}
Against the discrete-event evaluator the analytical model attains Spearman's $\rho$ of
$0.92$, $0.92$ and $0.96$ for telemetry, control and sensing, with MAPE of $39\%$, $44\%$
and $14\%$. On the containerised testbed (Table~\ref{tab:testbed},
Fig.~\ref{fig:model_vs_measured}) the pooled prediction error over the 36 architectures
is $28.7\%$ (MAPE), with a median absolute error of $7.5$\,ms, and the pooled rank
correlation is $\rho=0.97$, $\tau=0.89$. The decision-relevant metric is the pairwise
ordering agreement: among the $498$ architecture pairs that the model separates by more
than $25\%$, the measured order agrees in $98.2\%$ of the cases; among the $561$ pairs
separated by more than $10\%$, in $96.8\%$.

Two systematic effects are visible. For the data-intensive sensing workload the model
\emph{over}-predicts latency by roughly $30\%$ on eight of twelve architectures; because
the bias is systematic rather than random, the rank correlation for that workload is the
highest of the three ($\rho=0.95$) while its MAPE is the worst ($50\%$). And four
architectures --- all sensing designs with $\rho\ge0.75$ --- were not reproducible across
repeats, varying by factors of two to five (for instance $157$, $293$ and $556$\,ms
against a predicted $82$\,ms). Near saturation neither the analytical model nor the
deployment itself is stable, which is a result about the limits of design-time
prediction rather than measurement noise to be filtered out.

\subsection{A model correction found by the measurements}
The first version of the analytical model used an M/M/1 waiting term. Because stage
execution is deterministic, that formulation overestimates waiting by a factor of two,
and replacing it with the M/D/1 expression of \eqref{eq:latency} reduced the MAPE from
$28.9\%$ to $23.7\%$ on an identical set of measurements. Interestingly the rank
correlation was slightly \emph{higher} for the M/M/1 variant ($0.918$ versus $0.889$ on
that testbed configuration), i.e.\ accuracy and ordering do not improve in lockstep --- a
practical reminder that a model tuned for absolute prediction is not automatically the
better model for exploration.
