Hierarchical Constraint-Aware Design-Space Exploration for
Heterogeneous Distributed Cyber-Physical Systems
Nikita Tarasova, Vasyl Tomyuka
aLviv Polytechnic National University, Lviv, Ukraine
Abstract
Architecting a heterogeneous distributed cyber-physical system requires simultaneous decisions on
the number and class of edge and gateway nodes, on where application stages are executed, on
replication, and on communication links, under conflicting latency, energy, cost and network-volume
objectives. The resulting design space grows combinatorially, and for distributed cyber-physical
systems a single design point is expensive to evaluate, so exhaustive exploration is impractical while
population-based search returns approximate fronts without guarantees. This paper presents HCA-
DSE, a hierarchical constraint-aware design-space exploration method that discards structurally ill-
formed, provably infeasible and provably dominated regions of the architecture space without fully
evaluating each discarded candidate. Its central technical ingredient is a family of objective-specific
admissible relaxations for a finite heterogeneous edge–cloud CPS model with contention-dependent
objectives: the end-to-end latency of a distributed cyber-physical system depends on tier utilisation,
which is not determined by any single design decision, so a sum of assigned decision costs is not by
itself an admissible bound. We prove that the resulting pruning is safe and that the exact Pareto
front is preserved, and we verify both the bound admissibility and the front equality exhaustively on
reference spaces. Across four design-space scales and a scalability sweep up to 8.3 × 107 candidate
architectures, the default method performs 16–61 full evaluations.
It preserves the exhaustive
analytical Pareto front on the reference spaces S1–S3; exactness on the largest spaces rests on the
bound proof. Budget-matched NSGA-II attains median Pareto recall of 0.50–0.93 at 1000 actual
evaluator calls. An evaluation-cost model anchored at a measured DES cost of 32.8 ms projects
speed-ups up to 771×; this is not an end-to-end DES search measurement. We additionally isolate
variable-order and queue-bound effects and compare against an exact finite-table SMT baseline. An
independent containerised validation covers 36 architectures and 108 runs with the model frozen.
For architecture pairs separated by more than 25% in predicted latency, agreement with measured
mean-latency order is 88.6–91.3% within workloads (97.8% pooled).

1. Introduction
Distributed cyber-physical systems (dCPS) are increasingly deployed on a heterogeneous com-
pute continuum that spans sensing devices, edge nodes, gateways and cloud infrastructure. De-
signing such a system is not a single decision but a joint one: how many edge nodes and gateways

to deploy, which hardware class each of them should be, where each application stage is executed,
how far the ingest stage is replicated, and which class of communication link connects the tiers.
These decisions interact. Adding edge nodes reduces queueing delay but raises deployment cost and
static energy; moving analytics to the cloud removes load from the edge tier but adds a wide-area
transfer to every request; replication duplicates ingest execution and multiplies both compute load
and synchronisation traffic.
Because the decisions are discrete and interacting, the number of candidate architectures grows
as the product of the decision domains and quickly reaches 105 to 107 even for a modest set of design
variables. At the same time, evaluating a single candidate is expensive: a recent industrial dCPS
exploration methodology reports that “evaluating each design point takes under a minute” with a
discrete-event simulator, and its authors explicitly leave the integration of a search strategy out of
scope [1]. A survey of the field concludes that “efficient and scalable DSE technology for dCPS is
more or less non-existing and constitutes a largely unchartered research area” and identifies the cost
of a design-point evaluation as a defining difficulty of the distributed setting [2]. The combination
of a large space and an expensive evaluator is precisely the regime in which the number of full
evaluations, rather than the raw size of the space, determines whether design-space exploration
(DSE) is practical at all.
Two families of approaches dominate. Exhaustive or model-based enumeration evaluates ev-
ery candidate and therefore yields the exact Pareto set, but its cost scales with the size of the
space.
Population-based metaheuristics such as NSGA-II [3] operate under a fixed evaluation
budget and return an approximation with no guarantee on which trade-offs have been missed; Sec-
tion 6 measures its recall and hypervolume under precisely matched full-evaluator-call budgets. A
third, smaller family performs exact multi-objective exploration by pruning partial designs, either
symbolically with constraint solvers [4, 5] or through bound-based Pareto pruning in the broader
optimisation literature [6, 7]. Assignment-monotone partial costs are one way to obtain admissible
bounds, but exact bound-based search does not require all objectives to be assignment monotone.
Consistent approximations have also been used to avoid expensive DSE evaluations while retaining
exactness [8].
Here we study a concrete contention-dependent architecture model. Latency contains a waiting
component governed jointly by node counts, resource classes, replication and placement. We derive
objective-specific relaxations for this model and quantify their effect on exploration. The contribu-
tion is the model, bound construction and empirical analysis, rather than a new general principle
of safe Pareto pruning.

2. Related work
2.1. Exact multi-objective design-space exploration
The closest methodological relatives of this work perform exact multi-objective exploration by
reasoning about partial designs. Neubauer et al. [4] encode system-level synthesis in Answer Set
Programming modulo Theories and attach theory propagators that check both constraints and
Pareto dominance on incomplete assignments, returning conflict clauses that remove infeasible and
dominated regions early. Their formulation targets heterogeneous multiprocessors with routing,
optimising latency, area and energy, and their pruning is sound for problems whose objectives are
assignment monotonous. In the broader optimisation literature, multi-objective A∗with admissible
or consistent heuristics [9, 7] provides its theoretical foundation for bound-based pruning: a partial
solution whose optimistic vector is dominated by a complete solution can be discarded without
losing Pareto-optimal completions.
HCA-DSE belongs to this family, and we do not claim safe pruning as such as a contribution. The
difference is the class of objectives. In a distributed CPS the dominant latency term is contention-
dependent: it is a function of tier utilisation, which no single assignment determines. We therefore
construct bounds that are admissible without assignment monotonicity, by combining a term-wise
relaxation with an explicit decision ordering that makes the contention term exactly computable as
soon as its determinants are fixed (Section 4). Avoiding expensive evaluations is also an explicit aim
of consistent-approximation DSE [8], which combines safe approximations with exact assessment
of promising designs.
We therefore do not claim evaluator avoidance as a new principle.
Our
study specialises bounds to this CPS model and measures the evaluation-cost trade-off. The term
“Pareto pruning” also refers to selecting a representative subset of an already computed front [6];
that operation differs from our exact search-space pruning.
2.2. Design-space exploration for distributed cyber-physical systems
Herget et al. [2] survey DSE for dCPS and identify the two premises of this paper: design
spaces that are far larger than in classical MPSoC exploration, and design-point evaluations that
are considerably more expensive. CompDSE [1] uses Y-chart modelling [10] and derives workload,
3

platform and mapping models from execution traces of an industrial lithography machine, evaluating
design points with OMNeT++ [11]. It is an evaluator, not a search strategy: the authors report a
manual exploration of three variations and state that integrating search algorithms is out of scope.
The two approaches are therefore complementary — CompDSE answers how a design point should
be evaluated, HCA-DSE answers which design points need not be evaluated at all — and the per-
design-point cost reported there falls in the regime where our crossover analysis predicts speed-ups
of two orders of magnitude.
ContrArc [12] explores CPS architectures with a mixed-integer program combined with contract
refinement checking, pruning through subgraph isomorphism. It minimises a single objective (cost)
subject to requirements rather than constructing a Pareto front, and its pruning is constraint-based
rather than bound-based.
Simulation-based CPS exploration also includes the cyber/control co-design approach of Amir
and Givargis [13]. Automated workload derivation for industrial dCPS [14] addresses the input-
model construction problem. Edge CNN exploration [15] and its robustness extensions [16] illustrate
application-specific architecture trade-offs at the edge. These studies motivate broader workloads
and reliability models; they do not supply the objective bounds proved here.
2.3. Exact allocation by integer programming
A second exact family formulates allocation as an integer program and solves it to optimality.
Kouloumpris et al. [17] allocate the tasks of an application over an edge/hub/cloud triple with
binary integer linear programming, minimising latency or energy under computation, communica-
tion, memory and energy constraints, and validate on a UAV inspection use case with synthetic
benchmarks of up to 1000 tasks. Their follow-up [18] extends the formulation to a reliability/latency
problem solved exactly and offline for specified objective weights. This certifies a weighted optimum,
not enumeration of the entire discrete Pareto front.
These works are exact in a different sense than the present paper, and the distinction matters
for the choice of method. They solve one allocation instance over a fixed infrastructure with a
model that is linear in the decision variables, whereas we explore an architecture space in which the
number and class of nodes, the link class and the replication factor are themselves decisions, and
in which the latency model contains a non-linear contention term. A compact mixed-integer linear
formulation would require an encoding of the queueing term. Piecewise-linear approximations can
change the objective, but finite-domain table or disjunctive encodings can in principle retain the
exact values at the cost of model size. Nonlinearity therefore does not rule out an exact solver
baseline. Section 6.7 measures a finite-table SMT baseline, including table construction; a compact
symbolic encoding remains outside the comparison.
2.4. Design-time placement in the edge–cloud continuum
SPACE4AI-D [19] is the closest work in terms of the problem setting: design-time selection
of resources and component placement across edge, cloud and function-as-a-service layers. The
problem is formulated as a mixed-integer non-linear program with a single objective (deployment
cost) under response-time constraints, and solved with randomised greedy, local search, tabu search,
simulated annealing and genetic algorithms; no exactness guarantee is provided. Its validation on
a real deployment reports deviations of 12% in cost and 20% in execution time, which is a useful
reference point for the accuracy reported in Section 6.

2.5. Metaheuristic exploration of edge–cloud deployments
A large body of work applies evolutionary and swarm metaheuristics to task allocation, of-
floading and service placement in edge–cloud systems, typically optimising combinations of latency,
energy and cost. These methods are approximate by construction and their quality is a function of
the evaluation budget. We use NSGA-II [3] as implemented in pymoo [26] as the budget-matched
representative of this family, and report hypervolume [27], IGD+ [28] and Pareto recall against the
exhaustive ground truth; the choice of indicators follows the guidance of Audet et al. [29]. Our
conclusion is not that such methods are inferior in general, but that when admissible bounds are
available exactness is achievable at a small fraction of their evaluation budget.

3. System and design-space model
3.1. Architecture template
We consider the four-tier template of Fig. 1. A population of devices generates requests; each
request traverses three application stages. The pre-processing stage τ1 runs either on the edge tier
or on the gateway tier, the aggregation stage τ2 always runs on the gateway tier, and the analytics
stage τ3 runs either on the edge tier or in the cloud. Which of these variants is instantiated is a
design decision (the placement policy).
A compute node class is a tuple (C, M, P idle, P peak, c) of compute capacity [MIPS], memory
[MB], idle and peak power [W] and normalised cost per node. A link class is a tuple (B, τ prop, e, c)
of bandwidth [Mb/s], propagation delay [s], transfer energy [J/MB] and cost per attached node.
The gateway-to-cloud connection is fixed infrastructure (B = 200 Mb/s, τ prop = 20 ms) and is not
a design variable. Table 2 lists the concrete classes used in the experiments.

4.2. Admissible bounds
Definition 2 (Admissibility). LBi is admissible for objective fi if LBi(p) ≤fi(x) for every partial
architecture p and every x ∈C(p).
The partial-cost construction discussed by Neubauer et al. assumes that objectives are assign-
ment monotonous, i.e. that every assignment can only increase the objective, so that the partial
cost is itself a bound [4]. The waiting term of (3) violates this assumption: it depends on ρt, which
by (2) is jointly determined by nt, the class of the tier, r and π, and which decreases when nodes
are added. We therefore construct the bounds in two steps.

Proof. For every x ∈C(p) and every t, φt(p) ≤φt(x) by definition of the minimum. Summing over
t ∈Ti yields LBi(p) ≤fi(x).
The minima are taken independently per term, so the combination of values that realises them
need not correspond to any single completion; the bound is therefore optimistic but valid. The im-
plementation uses domain extrema for service, communication, static energy and cost, and minima
of class-specific power-to-capacity ratios for dynamic energy. Categorical policies are enumerated
over their small reachable set; they have no meaningful monotone endpoint order. Independent
extrema can relax a term further than its exact minimum, which remains safe. The queueing con-
tribution is bounded by zero until its determinants are fixed. Domain aggregates are precomputed
and repeated class-dependent terms cached; cache misses can still require scans of reachable classes
or policies.
Lemma 2 (Exactness on determined terms). Let supp(φt) be the set of variables φt depends on.
If supp(φt) ⊆{v1, . . . , vd} then φt is constant on C(p), and substituting its exact value for φt(p)
preserves admissibility while tightening the bound.
Proof. Constancy is immediate, and a constant equals its own minimum over C(p), so Lemma 1
applies unchanged.
Remark 1 (Why the variable order matters). Once all determinants of a tier’s waiting contribution
are assigned, that contribution is constant on C(p) and can enter the bound exactly by Lemma 2;
before then, zero is admissible for stable completions. If a determined used tier has ρt ≥1, its
latency is infinite. The default order O1 is (ne, κe, ng, κg, ℓ, κc, r, π). Policy is assigned last, so
its exact queueing contribution becomes available only at depth eight.

Remark 2. Structural invariants define what counts as an architecture rather than following from
the constraints of the problem: the evaluator can formally compute objectives for, say, a replicated
deployment under a policy that does not place the ingest stage on the edge tier, although such a
configuration is meaningless. Structural pruning is therefore part of the definition of the search
space, and the exhaustive baseline applies the same predicate. This distinction was found by the
test described in Section 5, which initially — and incorrectly — verified structural pruning as a
feasibility argument.
4.4. Algorithm
Algorithm 1 traverses the prefix tree depth-first. At every node the three rules are applied in
increasing order of cost: structural validity, then feasibility bounds, then dominance against an
archive of non-dominated evaluated points. A leaf that survives is evaluated in full; feasible leaves
update the archive. Fig. 2 illustrates the two bound-based rules.
4.5. Correctness
Theorem 1 (Pareto-front preservation). If all bounds are admissible, pruning is performed only by
Propositions 1–3, and the archive contains only feasible fully evaluated architectures, then the set of
non-dominated vectors returned by Algorithm 1 equals the true Pareto front: PHCA = P⋆.
Proof. (⊆) Every returned vector is feasible. If a returned vector were dominated by any feasible
vector, finiteness of X implies that it is dominated by a Pareto-optimal vector. The following argu-
ment shows that every Pareto-optimal vector is evaluated; it would therefore remove the dominated
vector during final filtering, a contradiction.

5. Experimental methodology
5.1. Research questions
RQ1 By how much does HCA-DSE reduce the number of full design-point evaluations, and how is
that reduction distributed over the pruning layers?
RQ2 Is the exact Pareto front preserved with respect to exhaustive enumeration?
RQ3 At which evaluator cost does the pruning overhead pay for itself?
RQ4 How does HCA-DSE compare with budget-matched stochastic exploration?
RQ5 How do the reduction and the runtime scale with the size of the design space?
RQ6 Do the architecture rankings produced by the analytical model transfer to a real containerised
edge–cloud deployment?
5.2. Design of the experiments
Four design-space scales (S1–S4, Table 4) are combined with the three workload classes of
Table 3. Exhaustive ground truth is computed for S1–S3; S4 and the scalability study are explored
by HCA-DSE only. Search counts are deterministic for a fixed traversal order. Stochastic baselines
use 20 independent seeds; DES and deployment measurements have separately specified repetitions.
Baselines. Exhaustive enumeration provides the ground-truth Pareto front. Random search and
NSGA-II [3] (pymoo [26], population 40, SBX crossover, polynomial mutation, integer repair) are
run under matched evaluation budgets of 100, 250, 500 and 1000 full evaluations, 20 seeds each.
All methods use exactly the same evaluator and feasibility rules. A proposal rejected structurally
consumes no full-evaluation budget. Every invocation on a structurally valid design consumes one
unit, including invocations returning infeasibility and repeated designs. The stopping condition is
exactly NE = B, also for a partial final NSGA-II generation. We persist proposal counts, structural
rejections, feasibility rejections and full evaluator calls; runtime includes proposal generation and
rejection. Duplicate handling remains method-specific: random sampling is with replacement, while
NSGA-II eliminates duplicates within its population.
12

Metrics. Pareto recall and precision are computed on sets of objective vectors against the exhaus-
tive front, identifying objective vectors at nine decimal places for these quality metrics. Exactness
experiments additionally compare unrounded binary64 objective-vector sets. Hypervolume [27] is
normalised by the hypervolume of the exhaustive front (HV ratio); IGD+ [28] uses the exhaustive
front as reference. We additionally report the number of full evaluations NE, the number of ex-
panded partial states N (recursive visits), and wall-clock time. Stochastic baselines are compared
with the Mann–Whitney U test [35] and Cliff’s δ [36]. The Mann–Whitney alternative is that
NSGA-II has larger HV values than random search; positive δ favours NSGA-II. Reported p-values
are unadjusted and comparisons are exploratory.
Ablation. Four variants isolate the contribution of each layer: structural only; structural + feasi-
bility bounds; structural + dominance bounds; and the full method. A separate 2 × 2 experiment
crosses orders O1/O2 (Remark 1) with the queueing bound enabled/disabled on S1–S3. We record
expanded states, bound calls, full evaluations, and exact front equality. An untimed instrumented
pass counts incomplete prefixes with a positive queueing contribution and those pruned only because
of that contribution against the same current archive. Runtime is the median of three uninstru-
mented runs.
Finite-table exact baseline. On S1/S2 we also use Z3 [37] to enumerate non-dominated points of
an exact finite relation. Every structurally valid design is first evaluated; feasible objective vectors
are encoded by per-objective integer ranks preserving the strict ordering of the stored binary64
values, with no linearisation or rounding. Repeated dominance queries find a non-dominated point
and block its dominated region until UNSAT. Reported time includes all evaluator calls, table
construction, encoding and solver queries.

5.3. Two-level evaluation and the cost model
The analytical evaluator of Section 3 (level A) is used for pruning, ground truth and all exactness
claims. A discrete-event simulator (level B) provides a higher-fidelity, and therefore more expensive,
evaluator: multi-server queues with a finite number of servers per tier, explicit link contention,
deterministic service times and Poisson arrivals. Replicated first-stage jobs fork and join; arrivals
after propagation are scheduled before claiming a server. The cloud and WAN each have one server.
We time 40 feasible S1/telemetry designs, each with 3000 requests and 400 warm-up requests, and
use their mean cost (32.8 ms) as a hardware- and sample-specific anchor. Search overheads in the
projection are medians of three runs. Model validation uses 40 distinct S2 designs per workload and
five DES seeds, reporting the median simulated mean latency. DES is a separate queueing model;
its front is not covered by the analytical exactness proof.
5.4. Testbed
The physical validation runs the architecture template as containers (Fig. 3). Each edge node,
gateway and cloud instance is a separate container limited to one CPU, so that a node behaves as
the single server assumed by the model. Services are implemented in Go and share one binary: a
request carries a plan listing the remaining stages, and each node consumes CPU work equal to
wi/Ct before forwarding the remainder together with the real payload to the next hop. Links are
shaped per node with tc netem [38] using the delay and rate of the link class of the design under
test; the gateway-to-cloud hop uses the WAN profile. Replication is emulated faithfully: r replicas

5.5. Reproducibility
The implementation is in Python 3 with NumPy, SciPy, pymoo and Z3; the testbed services are
in Go and run under Docker Compose. All figures and all numeric tables of this paper are generated
from the canonical v1.1-submission-results data; narrative numerical summaries are generated
from the same files. The release records a clean source export, locked package versions, execution
logs and SHA-256 hashes of sources and results. Archived CSVs are excluded from manuscript gen-
eration. A fresh interpreter rechecks search counters, budget identities, front equality and statistical
summaries. Wall times and deployment measurements are not expected to reproduce bit for bit.
Correctness is machine-checked at three levels: (i) bound admissibility is verified exhaustively on
S1, i.e. for every prefix and every completion of that prefix across the three workloads; (ii) for every
pruned prefix, all of its completions are enumerated and the claim of the corresponding proposition
is verified directly, on S1 and S2; and (iii) the equality PHCA = P⋆is re-checked on S1–S3 for every
pruning variant as a regression test.

6.10. Independent validation after model correction
Earlier deployment measurements motivated replacing an M/M/1-inspired waiting term with the
M/D/1-inspired expression. This historical change was post hoc; the independent round reported
here uses a frozen model and is not fitted to its observations. Deterministic service motivates the
factor of two only in an idealised single-server queue. It does not establish an exact queueing formula
for the full deployment, so we retain the measured errors and unstable repeats as limitations rather
than adjusting the model after validation.
7. Discussion
7.1. Why hierarchical pruning works here
The ablation shows that structural restrictions, feasibility bounds and an incumbent Pareto
archive provide complementary reductions on these design spaces. The default search does not
need early queueing contributions to obtain the reported evaluation savings. The separate order
experiment (Section 6.6) measures the effect of assigning their determinants earlier; it also makes
clear that changing order changes which incumbents become available for dominance pruning.
7.2. When HCA-DSE is the right tool — and when it is not
The method is advantageous when the design space is large, the constraints are tight, the
evaluator is expensive, and admissible bounds can be constructed. The crossover analysis quantifies
23

the third condition using actual exhaustive evaluator calls and measured overhead differences. At
the measured DES cost it projects up to 771× speed-up, but does not execute DES-based search
or establish its exactness. Industrial evaluation costs can be substantially higher [1]; transfer of
the numerical speed-ups nevertheless requires new measurements and bounds appropriate to that
evaluator.
Conversely, the method offers little when the space is small, the constraints are loose, the
evaluation is nearly free, or — most importantly — when no admissible bound can be constructed
for the objectives of interest. If a bound cannot be proven admissible it must not be used: the
guarantee of Theorem 1 rests entirely on that property, and an inadmissible bound silently converts
an exact method into an approximate one without any indication in the output.
7.3. Relation to symbolic exact exploration
Symbolic approaches [4] prune partial designs with a constraint solver and are, in principle,
more general than closed-form bounds: any constraint the solver can express can participate in
the pruning. The assignment-monotone construction of the cited method does not directly supply
our queueing bounds. Our construction instead uses objective-specific relaxations. These bounds
preserve the analytical front, although the default order activates the exact queueing term only at
full assignment. The order ablation tests earlier activation. Our finite-table Z3 baseline is exact but
builds all evaluator values first; a compact solver front end with partial-design propagation remains
an untested alternative.
7.4. Relation to evaluator-centric methodologies
CompDSE [1] and HCA-DSE answer different questions. The former asks how a design point
of an industrial dCPS can be modelled and evaluated faithfully; the latter asks which design points
need not be evaluated at all. Their combination is conditional: analytical bounds would need to be
proved admissible for the trace-derived simulator itself. Admissibility for the analytical model and
good empirical rank agreement do not establish that condition. The crossover analysis is a cost
projection and supplies no simulator-level exactness guarantee.
7.5. Accuracy versus ordering
The independent validation assesses both prediction error and ordering. Within-workload agree-
ment with measured mean-latency order is 88.6–91.3% on pairs separated by more than 25% in
predicted latency; the pooled value is 97.8%. Pooling can benefit from workload separation and
should not be read as evidence that every local design choice is reliable. Repeated runs also expose
instability that a fixed analytical objective cannot represent.
The theorem guarantees the Pareto front of the analytical model.
It does not imply that
this front equals the deployment or DES front. Analytical exploration can select candidates for
higher-fidelity reassessment, but discarding all other candidates preserves that evaluator’s front
only if the pruning bounds are also proved admissible for it. The measured-mean and measured-p50
comparisons are reported separately because an expected-latency approximation does not directly
predict a latency quantile.
24

8. Threats to validity
Internal validity. The correctness of the method rests on the admissibility of the bounds. We ad-
dress this at three levels: proofs (Lemmas 1–2, Propositions 1–3, Theorem 1); an exhaustive machine
check of LBi(p) ≤fi(x) over all prefixes and all completions on S1 for the three workloads; and a
direct check that, for every pruned prefix, no completion contradicts the claim of the corresponding
rule. We deliberately separate theoretical correctness from implementation correctness and verify
both, since an admissible bound that is implemented incorrectly is indistinguishable, in the output,
from an inadmissible one. Structural pruning is part of the definition of the design space rather
than a consequence of the constraints, and the exhaustive baseline uses the same predicate; this
was discovered by the pruning-safety test and is stated explicitly rather than absorbed into the
comparison.
Post-hoc model correction. Earlier measurements motivated the M/D/1-inspired waiting correction.
The independent second round freezes this model and all 36 configurations beforehand, so its results
are not in-sample calibration scores. Selection of these configurations still reflects the original study,
and a single-machine repeat is not an independent deployment population. The idealised queueing
argument does not determine the sign of deployment error.
Construct validity. The analytical model uses per-node service times with a stage-wise M/D/1-
inspired waiting multiplier based on average tier utilisation. It is neither an exact aggregate-server
queue nor an exact multi-server queue, and its error has no proved sign. Transport protocol effects
are ignored, the power model is linear in utilisation, and costs are normalised units.
Testbed
validation quantifies latency error and ordering agreement, but does not prove admissibility for
a simulator or for the deployment. The model omits transport-level and host-scheduling effects,
which can be especially consequential for large payloads and near saturation. The energy and cost
models are not validated against measurements at all; they are used for comparative exploration
only, and no absolute energy or cost claim is made.
External validity. The workloads are synthetic, although chosen to be qualitatively different rather
than rescaled versions of one another, and the architecture template is fixed to four tiers with three
stages.
The empirical study is further restricted to configurations that fit on a single machine
(≤4 edge nodes, ≤2 gateways), and link shaping is applied in the forward direction of each hop,
matching the one-way transfers counted by the model. Results on a geographically distributed
deployment may differ, particularly for the WAN hop.
Conclusion validity. Search counts are deterministic for fixed inputs and order; wall-clock timings
still vary. Stochastic baselines use 20 seeds and exploratory, unadjusted Mann–Whitney tests with
Cliff’s δ. Testbed configurations have only three repeats. We report the median of run means,
repeat IQR and a largest/smallest ratio; unstable configurations remain in the main results. This
sample does not characterise tail-risk probabilities.
Evaluation-budget accounting. All stochastic runs stop at exactly B full evaluator calls, with struc-
turally rejected proposals counted separately. Calls returning infeasibility consume budget, as do
repeat evaluations. Thus budgets match full invocations rather than unique feasible designs or
wall-clock work. NSGA-II duplicate elimination and random sampling with replacement remain
distinct search choices, not equal unique-design budgets.
25

Limitations. The method assumes a static workload during one exploration, does not model runtime
migration or autoscaling, and requires bounds to be derived per objective — a manual step that
must be repeated when the objective set changes. The finite-table Z3 baseline on S1/S2 is an exact
encoding, but fully evaluates the space before solving. It does not establish superiority over compact
SMT, CP-SAT or mixed-integer formulations with native propagation. Exact integer allocation is
practical for related problems [17, 18]; a comparable compact encoding of this architecture model
remains future work. These limits constrain claims of general algorithmic efficiency beyond the
measured competitors.
9. Conclusions
In the class of heterogeneous dCPS design spaces studied here, exploration cost is strongly
driven by full design-point evaluation cost as well as combinatorial space size. HCA-DSE combines
structural, feasibility and dominance pruning with objective-specific admissible relaxations for a
finite edge–cloud architecture model. The construction preserves the exact analytical Pareto front;
safe pruning and evaluator avoidance are established principles, while the contribution here is their
concrete bound construction and experimental assessment for this model.
The default method performs 16–61 full evaluations across the reported spaces, including a
sweep up to 8.3×107 raw candidates. Exhaustive reference comparisons on S1–S3 recover identical
unrounded objective-vector sets.
Stochastic baselines now spend precisely their stated full-call
budgets; at B = 1000, NSGA-II median recall ranges from 0.50–0.93. The cost model projects
up to 771× speed-up using the measured 32.8 ms DES cost, without claiming an end-to-end DES
search measurement. The order/queue ablation distinguishes early bound activation from traversal
effects, and the finite-table SMT comparison provides a small-space exact reference whose table-
construction cost is explicitly included.
The independent Docker round uses 36 fixed architectures and 108 runs without refitting the
model. Agreement with measured mean-latency order is 88.6–91.3% within workloads for pairs
separated by more than 25% in the model, compared with 97.8% pooled. These measurements
assess transfer of analytical rankings; they neither validate energy/cost objectives nor establish
deployment-level Pareto exactness. Further work should develop compact symbolic solver encodings
and tighter bounds proved admissible for higher-fidelity evaluators.
